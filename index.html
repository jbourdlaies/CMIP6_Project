CMIP6 Global Temperature Explorer

Comparing pre-industrial (950-1450) control and historical (1850-2015) climate simulations using ACCESS-CM2.

Temperature anomalies are calculated relative to each experiment's own reference period.

Project Write-up

Design Rationale

Our visualization uses an interactive trendline graph to compare global temperature anomalies from 2 CMIP6 climate experiments, piControl and historical, using the ACCESS-CM2 climate model.

We experimented with other graphs such as bar charts, heatmaps, categorical dot plots, and single line charts. In the end, an interactive trendline graph featuring two separate lines was selected because it effectively communicates changes in temperature over time and makes it easier to identify distinct long-term trends and variation between the two experiments. Simulation year is displayed along the horizontal axis while global temperature anomaly (°C) is displayed along the vertical axis.

Different colors were assigned to each experiment to make the two datasets visually distinguishable. Blue represents the pre-industrial piControl simulation, while red represents the historical simulation. These colors provide a clear visual contrast without making the graph unnecessarily complicated.

The checkboxes at the top allow users to independently show or hide either experiment. This provides flexibility when examining individual trends or comparing both simulations at the same time. A hover interaction was also implemented to display the nearest data point's experiment, year, and temperature anomaly.

As we learned in DSC 10, the line graph is ultimately among the best visualizations to represent data that takes place over a continuous time dimension and showcase trends and fluctuations.

Development Process

The application was developed using HTML, CSS, JavaScript, and the D3.js visualization library. HTML was used to establish the page structure, CSS was used for styling and layout, and D3.js was used to load, process, and visualize the CMIP6 temperature anomaly dataset.

The development process involved preparing the CMIP6 dataset based on Sam Lau's approach, selecting parameters, experimenting with different graphs using the data, implementing the final dynamic graph, configuring the axes and scales, adding interactive features, and deploying the final website. Particular attention was given to ensure that both experiments could be compared clearly despite their different simulation periods.

The project took approximately 4 people and took roughly 1 week to complete. While we did not keep track of hours, it is most likely that we spent an average of an hour per day each doing our share of work. The most time-consuming aspect of development was the experimentation process, as deciding what graphs to use and coding them to see how they would turn out while also navigating the large dataset proved to be a challenge initially.

Overall, the development process provided an opportunity to work with climate simulation data and explore how interactive data visualization can make complex scientific datasets more accessible and understandable.
